\title[Peterson elements and the Dickson--M\`ui algebras]{Peterson elements and the Steenrod action\\ on the Dickson--M\`ui algebras}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\sloppy

\begin{abstract}
We describe the algorithm used in \code{yacop/modules/dickson.py} to compute
the action of the mod $p$ Steenrod algebra on the Dickson algebras $D_k$
(and on their M\`ui extensions).  The algorithm rests on a family of
invariants, the \emph{Peterson elements} $\wt_n$, and on a closed formula
$P^R\wt_n = a(R,n)\,\wt_{n+d(R)}$ for the action of a Milnor basis element on
them.  We define the Peterson elements, give an explicit monomial formula for
them which generalises a formula of Hung and Peterson, and prove that the
Dickson invariants are Peterson elements.  We extract the action formula from
the code.  For odd $p$ it holds with the normalisation $\wt_n=(-1)^n\omega_n$,
not with the one stated in the code's documentation.  We prove the formula
for all Peterson elements, all $k$ and all Milnor basis elements.  In
particular the algorithm is correct.  We also prove the code's formula for
the conjugate action.  Using two-sided Peterson elements, which for $p=2$ are
Arnon's generalised Dickson invariants, we show that the $\GL_k$-invariant
Peterson elements are exactly the Lagrange elements that are polynomials.  Finally we explain the auxiliary class
\code{DicksonDualSteenrodAlgebra}.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{Introduction}

The program \code{yacop} computes the action of the Steenrod algebra $\A$ on
the Dickson algebras
$\Dk=H^*(BV_k;\F)^{\GL_k}$, where $V_k=\F^k$.  Here $\Dk$ is viewed as an
algebra of invariants in the polynomial part of $H^*(BV_k)$.  Since $\Dk$ is a
polynomial algebra, the Cartan formula reduces the problem to the action on
the generators.  The code handles the generators by identifying each of them
with a \emph{Peterson element} and then using a closed formula for the Milnor
basis action on Peterson elements.  This note explains that formula and how
much of it can be proved.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Summary of results.}  Let $R=(r_1,r_2,\dots)$ index the
Milnor basis element $P^R$ (written $Sq(R)$ when $p=2$).  Put
\[
  |R|=\sum_j r_j,\qquad N(R)=\sum_j p^j r_j,\qquad
  d(R)=\sum_j r_j\,\frac{p^j-1}{p-1},
\]
so that $(p-1)d(R)=N(R)-|R|$.  For $n\ge 0$ define the coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aRn}
  a(R,n)\;=\;\binom{(p-1)(n+d(R))}{N(R)}\binom{N(R)}{pr_1,\,p^2r_2,\,p^3r_3,\,\dots}\in\F ,
\end{equation}
where the second factor is a multinomial coefficient.  The code (functions
\code{\_\_action\_exponents} and \code{\_\_action\_exponents2} in
\code{dickson.py}) uses
\[
   P^R\,\wt_n \;=\; a(R,n)\,\wt_{n+d(R)} .\tag{$\ast$}
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item Section~\ref{sec:peterson} defines Peterson elements and proves the
  monomial formula and the identification of the Dickson invariants
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:monomials}, Theorem~\ref{thm:A}).
\item Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} describes the algorithm.  The code's
  decomposition of Peterson elements into Dickson monomials is a Lagrange
  inversion formula (Proposition~\ref{prop:lagrange}).
\item Section~\ref{sec:proof} proves $(\ast)$ at the Dickson generators
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:gen}), which shows that the algorithm is correct
  (Corollary~\ref{cor:algo}).  It then proves $(\ast)$ in all degrees
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:all}), and the formula used by the code for the
  conjugate action (Theorem~\ref{thm:conjall}).  It identifies the
  $\Omega_n$ with two-sided Peterson elements (Theorem~\ref{thm:arnon}), on
  which the action formula has a three-line proof
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:twoaction}).  This proves Theorem~\ref{conj:B}.  Table~\ref{tab:checks} lists
  independent computer checks.
\item Section~\ref{sec:whole} explains how the code obtains the action on all
  of $\Dk$, Section~\ref{sec:mui} derives the action on the M\`ui generators,
  and Section~\ref{sec:dual} explains
  \code{DicksonDualSteenrodAlgebra}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[A sign]\label{rem:sign}
The code documents its Dickson generators as the Peterson elements
$\omega_n=(x_1\cdots x_k)\,\chi(P^n)\bigl((x_1\cdots x_k)^{-1}\bigr)$.  For odd
$p$, formula $(\ast)$ is \emph{false} for the $\omega_n$; for example
$P^1\omega_1=-\omega_2$ in $H^*(B\mathbb{Z}/3)$, while $(\ast)$ predicts
$+\omega_2$.  Formula $(\ast)$ is correct for
$\wt_n=(-1)^n\omega_n$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:all}, together with
Corollary~\ref{cor:algo} for the comparison of $\wt_n$ and $\Omega_n$).  Both the decomposition formula and the Cartan formula
are insensitive to this sign change: $n$ is additive under products, so a
Dickson monomial in the $\wt$'s picks up the same sign $(-1)^n$ as $\wt_n$
itself.  So the decomposition formula is the same for $\omega$ and $\wt$, and
the code's generators are consistent with $\wt_{n_j}$.  With that reading the
module computed by the code is the correct one.  Only the identification
``generator $=\omega_n$'' in the documentation should read
``generator $=\wt_n$''.  For $p=2$ there is no difference.
\end{remark}

\section{Recollections}\label{sec:recollections}

\subsection{Steenrod operations}
Write $H=\F[x_1,\dots,x_k]$, with $|x_i|=1$ for $p=2$ and $|x_i|=2$ for $p$
odd.  This is the polynomial part of $H^*(BV_k)$.  (For odd $p$,
$H^*(BV_k)=E(y_1,\dots,y_k)\otimes H$ with $|y_i|=1$, and the Bockstein maps
$y_i\mapsto x_i$.)  We use the Milnor basis $\{P^R\}$ of the subalgebra of
$\A$ generated by the reduced powers.  Let $t=(t_1,t_2,\dots)$ be indeterminates
and put $t_0=1$.  The \emph{total Milnor operation}
\[
  \Lambda_t(y)=\sum_R t^R P^R(y),\qquad t^R=\prod_j t_j^{r_j},
\]
is a ring homomorphism $H\to H[t]$, extended $\F[t]$-linearly.  It is
determined by
\[
  \Lambda_t(x)=T(x):=\sum_{j\ge0} t_j\,x^{p^j}\qquad(x\in H^{|x_1|}).
\]
Since the elements $\Lambda_t$ are group-like, the conjugate operation
$\Lambda'_t(y)=\sum_R t^R\chi(P^R)(y)$ is the inverse of $\Lambda_t$.  On a
degree-one (resp.\ degree-two) class it is $\Lambda'_t(x)=T^{-1}(x)$, where
$T^{-1}$ is the compositional inverse of the additive series $T$.
(Indeed $\Lambda_t\bigl(\sum_j c_jx^{p^j}\bigr)=\sum_j c_j T(x)^{p^j}$ for
scalars $c_j\in\F[t]$.)

\subsection{Dickson invariants}
Let $V\subset H$ be the $\F$-span of $x_1,\dots,x_k$.  The Dickson polynomial
\[
  f_V(X)\;=\;\prod_{v\in V}(X-v)\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^k c_{k,i}\,X^{p^i},\qquad c_{k,k}=1,
\]
is additive in $X$.  Its coefficients $c_{k,i}$ are the (signed) Dickson
invariants.  In the classical notation $c_{k,i}=(-1)^{k-i}Q_{k,i}$, and
$|c_{k,i}|=(p^k-p^i)|x_1|$.  Dickson's theorem says
$\Dk=H^{\GL_k}=\F[c_{k,0},\dots,c_{k,k-1}]$~\cite{Dickson}.  Two facts about
additive polynomials will be used repeatedly:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(D1)] An additive polynomial with $\F$-coefficients is $\F$-linear.  So it
  vanishes on $V$ as soon as it vanishes on $x_1,\dots,x_k$.
\item[(D2)] Over a domain, a monic polynomial of degree $p^k$ that vanishes on
  the $p^k$ distinct elements of $V$ equals $f_V$.
\end{itemize}
Since $\Lambda_t$ is a ring homomorphism,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lf}
  \Lambda_t(f_V)(X)=\prod_{v\in V}\bigl(X-T(v)\bigr)=f_{T(V)}(X).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The M\`ui extension}
For odd $p$, M\`ui~\cite{Mui} computed the full ring of invariants
$H^*(BV_k)^{\GL_k}$.  It is generated over $\Dk$ by exterior-type classes
$R_{k,i}$ ($0\le i<k$) of degree $2(p^k-1)-2p^i+1$, with $R_{k,i}^2=0$.  These
are built from Moore-type determinants with one row of $y$'s; see
also~\cite{KamekoMimura}.  The \code{DicksonAlgebra} class with
\code{generic=True} adds odd generators $\tau_i$ of degree $1-2p^i$ to $\Dk$.
These are the invariants $R_{k,i}/Q_{k,0}$ (up to sign).  Their action is
derived in Section~\ref{sec:mui}.  Apart from that section, this note
concentrates on the polynomial part~$\Dk$.

\section{Peterson elements}\label{sec:peterson}

Let $S(z)=\sum_{j\ge0}z^{p^j}$.  This is the compositional inverse of the
additive polynomial $z\mapsto z-z^p$, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S}
  S(z)-S(z)^p=z .
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}\label{def:peterson}
Let $e=x_1\cdots x_k$.  For $n\ge0$ the \emph{Peterson element}
$\omega_n\in H$ is
\[
  \omega_n=e\cdot\chi(P^n)(e^{-1}).
\]
Here $e^{-1}$ lives in the localisation $H[e^{-1}]$, on which $\A$ acts by the
Cartan formula.  The \emph{normalised} Peterson element is
$\wt_n=(-1)^n\omega_n$.  It has degree $(p-1)n|x_1|$.  (Code:
\code{PetersonPolynomials.omega}, with \code{\_omega\_safe} implementing the
definition.)
\end{definition}

For $p=2$ these are the elements studied by Arnon~\cite[Def.~3.2]{Arnon},
who attributes them to Peterson, and by Hung and
Peterson~\cite[\S2]{HungPeterson98}.  Arnon also allows negative powers of $2$
as exponents; see Section~\ref{sec:arnon}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:monomials}
\begin{enumerate}
\item In the completion of $H$,
\[
  \sum_{n\ge0}\wt_n\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^k\frac{x_i}{S(x_i)}\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^k\bigl(1-S(x_i)^{p-1}\bigr).
\]
\item Call $a\ge0$ \emph{admissible} if $a=0$ or the base-$p$ digit sum of $a$
is exactly $p-1$.  For admissible $a=\sum_j\varepsilon_jp^j$, put
$\varepsilon(a)!=\prod_j\varepsilon_j!$.  Then
\[
  \wt_n\;=\sum_{\substack{a_1+\dots+a_k=(p-1)n\\ a_i\ \text{admissible}}}
  \ \prod_{i=1}^k\frac{1}{\varepsilon(a_i)!}\;x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_k^{a_k}.
\]
For $p=2$ the admissible numbers are $0$ and the powers of $2$, and all
coefficients are $1$.  This recovers the Hung--Peterson formula.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) Apply $\Lambda'_t$ with $t=(-1,0,0,\dots)$.  Then
$T(x)=x-x^p$, so $\Lambda'_t(x)=T^{-1}(x)=S(x)$ by~\eqref{eq:S}.  Hence
$\sum_n(-1)^n\chi(P^n)(e^{-1})=\Lambda'_t(e^{-1})=\prod_iS(x_i)^{-1}$.  Multiply
by $e$ and take the homogeneous component of degree $(p-1)n|x_1|$.  The second
equality is $z/S(z)=(S(z)-S(z)^p)/S(z)=1-S(z)^{p-1}$.

(2) Expand $S(z)^{p-1}=\sum\binom{p-1}{\varepsilon_0,\varepsilon_1,\dots}
z^{\sum\varepsilon_jp^j}$ over $\sum\varepsilon_j=p-1$.  Different
$\varepsilon$ give different exponents, since there are no carries.  By Wilson's
theorem $-\binom{p-1}{\varepsilon}=-(p-1)!/\varepsilon!=1/\varepsilon!$ in
$\F$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
For $p=3$, $k=2$ we get $\wt_1=-(x_1^2+x_2^2)$ and
$\wt_3=-(x_1^6+x_1^4x_2^2+x_1^2x_2^4+x_2^6)=c_{2,1}$.  Also
$\wt_4=x_1^6x_2^2+x_1^4x_2^4+x_1^2x_2^6=c_{2,0}$.  The element $\wt_1$ is
not invariant.  In general $\wt_n$ need not be $\GL_k$-invariant.  For $p=2$,
$k=3$, the non-invariant ones with $n\le 40$ are $n=1,2,3,5,9,17,33$.  For $p=3$,
$k=2$ they are $n=1,2,5,14$.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Dickson invariants are Peterson elements]\label{thm:A}
For $0\le i\le k$,
\[
  c_{k,i}=\wt_{(p^k-p^i)/(p-1)} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $F(X)=\sum_{i=0}^k\wt_{(p^k-p^i)/(p-1)}X^{p^i}$.  It is additive with
$\F$-coefficients and monic of degree $p^k$, because $\wt_0=1$.  By (D1), (D2)
and symmetry it suffices to show $F(x_1)=0$.  Introduce a grading variable
$w$ and put $\wt(w)=\sum_n\wt_nw^{(p-1)n}=\prod_l(1-S(x_lw)^{p-1})$.  The
coefficient of $w^{p^k}$ in $\wt(w)S(x_1w)=\sum_{i\ge0}\wt(w)(x_1w)^{p^i}$
is $\sum_{i\le k}\wt_{(p^k-p^i)/(p-1)}x_1^{p^i}=F(x_1)$.  By~\eqref{eq:S},
\[
  \wt(w)\,S(x_1w)=\bigl(S(x_1w)-S(x_1w)^p\bigr)\prod_{l\ge2}\bigl(1-S(x_lw)^{p-1}\bigr)
  = x_1w\prod_{l=2}^k\bigl(1-S(x_lw)^{p-1}\bigr).
\]
Its $w^{p^k}$-coefficient is $x_1$ times a sum of monomials
$x_2^{a_2}\cdots x_k^{a_k}$ with admissible $a_l$ and $\sum a_l=p^k-1$.  The
digit sum is subadditive, so the digit sum of $\sum a_l$ is at most
$(k-1)(p-1)$.  But $p^k-1$ has digit sum $k(p-1)$.  So there are no such
monomials, and $F(x_1)=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For $p=2$, Theorem~\ref{thm:A} is~\cite[Prop.~3.6(c)]{Arnon} and the formula
of~\cite[\S2]{HungPeterson98}.
In the code, generator number $j$ of \code{DicksonAlgebra($p$,$k$)} (counted
from $1$, in the order \code{d\{deg\}} is listed) is $c_{k,k-j}=\wt_{n_j}$ with
\[
  n_j=p^{k-j}\,\frac{p^j-1}{p-1}=\frac{p^k-p^{k-j}}{p-1}.
\]
This is computed in \code{\_left\_steenrod\_coaction\_milnor\_gen}.  For example
\code{d4,d6,d7} for $p=2$, $k=3$ are $\wt_4,\wt_6,\wt_7$, and \code{d12,d16} for
$p=3$, $k=2$ are $\wt_3=c_{2,1}=-Q_{2,1}$ and $\wt_4=c_{2,0}=Q_{2,0}$.
\end{remark}

\section{The algorithm}\label{sec:algorithm}

\subsection{Decomposing Peterson elements}
Let $\Dperf$ be the perfect closure of $\Dk$, i.e.\ $\Dk$ with all $p$-power
roots of its elements adjoined.  Put
\[
  q_j=c_{k,k-j}^{\,p^{-(k-j)}}\in\Dperf\quad(0\le j\le k),\qquad
  \varphi(u)=\sum_{j=0}^k q_j\,u^{p^j},
\]
so $q_0=1$ and $q_k=c_{k,0}$.  Since $\varphi(u)=u+O(u^p)$ is additive, it has
an additive compositional inverse $\psi(w)=w+O(w^p)$.

\begin{definition}
For $n\ge0$ and $m=(p-1)n$ let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Omega}
  \Omega_n=\coef{u^m}\Bigl(\frac{u}{\varphi(u)}\Bigr)^{m}
  =\sum_{R}\binom{-m}{|R|}\binom{|R|}{r_1,r_2,\dots,r_k}\prod_{j=1}^k q_j^{\,r_j}\ \in\Dperf ,
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over $R=(r_1,\dots,r_k)$ with $\sum_jr_j(p^j-1)=m$.
\end{definition}

This is exactly what \code{PetersonPolynomials.decompose} and
\code{DicksonAlgebra.peterson} compute.  The Milnor basis enumerates the
exponent vectors $R$.  The term $q^R$ is the Dickson monomial
$\prod_j c_{k,k-j}^{\,r_j/p^{k-j}}$.  When a term with nonzero coefficient
has $p^{k-j}\nmid r_j$, the element lies in $\Dperf\setminus\Dk$ and the code
raises \code{ValueError}.  Terms with $r_j\ne0$ for some $j>k$ are dropped,
which is consistent with $q_j=0$ for $j>k$.

\begin{proposition}[Lagrange inversion]\label{prop:lagrange}
$\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\Omega_n\,w^{(p-1)n}=\frac{w}{\psi(w)}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Lagrange inversion in residue form holds over any commutative
ring~\cite{Gessel}.  It says $\coef{w^m}H(\psi(w))=\coef{u^m}H(u)\varphi'(u)(u/\varphi(u))^{m+1}$.
Here $\varphi'=1$ because $\varphi$ is additive.  Take $H(u)=\varphi(u)/u$.
Also, $u/\varphi(u)$ is a series in $u^{p-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:Aomega}
$\Omega_{n_j}=c_{k,k-j}$ for $0\le j\le k$.  In particular the generators
satisfy $\Omega_{n_j}=\wt_{n_j}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $\Omega(w)=w/\psi(w)$.  As in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:A},
$\sum_j\Omega_{n_j}X^{p^{k-j}}=\coef{w^{p^k}}\Omega(w)S(Xw)$.  With
$H(u)=\frac{\varphi(u)}{u}S(X\varphi(u))$, Lagrange inversion turns this into
\[
  \coef{u^{p^k}}\,S\bigl(X\varphi(u)\bigr)\Bigl(\frac{u}{\varphi(u)}\Bigr)^{p^k}
  =\coef{u^{p^k}}\,S\bigl(X\varphi(u)\bigr)\cdot\bigl(1+\textstyle\sum_{j\ge1}q_j^{p^k}u^{p^k(p^j-1)}\bigr)^{-1}.
\]
The second factor is $1$ plus terms of $u$-degree $\ge p^k$.  The first has no
constant term.  So only the constant $1$ contributes, and we get
$\coef{u^{p^k}}\sum_aX^{p^a}\varphi(u)^{p^a}=\sum_{a+j=k}q_j^{p^a}X^{p^a}=f_V(X)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{conj:B}
If $\wt_n$ is $\GL_k$-invariant, then $\wt_n=\Omega_n$.  This happens if and
only if $\sigma_p(n)\ge k$, where
$\sigma_p(n)=\alpha((p-1)n)/(p-1)+\nu_p(n)$, $\alpha$ is the base-$p$ digit sum
and $\nu_p$ is the $p$-adic valuation.
\end{theorem}

At the generator degrees $n=n_j$ this is Theorem~\ref{thm:Aomega}.  The general
case is proved in Section~\ref{sec:arnon} (Corollary~\ref{cor:conjB2}).  The
algorithm itself only needs the elements $\Omega_n$, so it does not depend on
Theorem~\ref{conj:B}.  That theorem only matters for relating the code's
\code{peterson(n)} to the polynomial $\wt_n$ in other degrees.

\subsection{The action on Peterson elements}
The coefficient $a(R,n)$ of~\eqref{eq:aRn} is what
\code{\_\_action\_exponents} computes for the ordinary action.  The recursion
\code{\_\_action\_exponents2} produces the multinomial
$\binom{N(R)}{pr_1,p^2r_2,\dots}$ as an iterated product of binomial
coefficients, and \code{binom\_modp(p, psum+n*(p-1)-esum, psum)} is
$\binom{(p-1)(n+d(R))}{N(R)}$.  The operation $\Lambda_t$ extends uniquely to
$\Dperf$ (with values in $\Dperf[t^{1/p^\infty}]$).  We let $P^R$ act on
$\Dperf$ by taking the coefficient of the integral monomial $t^R$.

\begin{theorem}[Action formula]\label{thm:C}
For all $k$, $n$ and $R$,
\[
  P^R\,\Omega_n=a(R,n)\,\Omega_{n+d(R)} .
\]
\end{theorem}

This is proved in Section~\ref{sec:proof} (Theorem~\ref{thm:all}).  The code
uses a second formula for the conjugate action (\code{isconjugate=True}),
proved in Theorem~\ref{thm:conjall}.

\begin{theorem}[Conjugate action]\label{thm:conjact}
For all $k$, $n$ and $R$,
\[
  \chi(P^R)\,\Omega_n=b(R,n)\,\Omega_{n+d(R)},\qquad
  b(R,n)=\binom{-(p-1)n}{N(R)}\binom{N(R)}{pr_1,p^2r_2,\dots}.
\]
\end{theorem}

The algorithm needs Theorem~\ref{thm:C} only at the generator degrees
$n=n_j$, where $\Omega_{n_j}=c_{k,k-j}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gen}
  P^R c_{k,k-j}=a(R,n_j)\,\Omega_{n_j+d(R)} .
\end{equation}
This case is proved in Theorem~\ref{thm:gen}.  If the coefficient is nonzero,
then $\Omega_{n_j+d(R)}=P^Rc_{k,k-j}/a(R,n_j)$ lies in $\Dk$, so the call to
\code{peterson} cannot fail.

\begin{example}
For $p=3$, $k=2$, $R=(0,1)$ and $n=n_1=3$ we have $d(R)=4$ and $N(R)=9$.  The
coefficient is $a(R,3)=\binom{14}{9}\equiv1$, so
$P(0,1)c_{2,1}=\Omega_7=c_{2,1}c_{2,0}$.  The code prints
\code{P(0,1)*d12 = d12*d16}.  For $R=(1)$ the coefficient is
$\binom{8}{3}\equiv2$, so $P^1c_{2,1}=2c_{2,0}$, matching
\code{P(1)*d12 = 2*d16}.  In classical notation this reads
$P^1Q_{2,1}=Q_{2,0}$.
\end{example}

\section{Proofs and evidence}\label{sec:proof}

\subsection{A generating-function form}
Let $s_j=t_j^{1/p^j}$ and let
\[
  T^*(w)=\sum_{j\ge0}(t_jw)^{p^{-j}}=w+\sum_{j\ge1}s_j\,w^{p^{-j}}
\]
be the adjoint of $T$.  We work with formal sums of monomials
$x^\alpha t^\epsilon w^\beta$ with $\epsilon\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p]_{\ge0}^{J}$ and
$\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p]$, where each $x$-degree has only finitely many terms.
These form a ring, and every expression below has only finitely many terms in
each $x$-degree.  Let $\pi$ be the projection onto monomials with integral
$t$-exponents.  It is linear over elements with integral $t$-exponents.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:G}
Let $(\Omega_M)_{M\ge0}$ be elements of degree $(p-1)M$ in a graded
commutative $\F$-algebra, and $\Omega(w)=\sum_M\Omega_Mw^{(p-1)M}$.  Then
\[
  \pi\Bigl[\sum_M\Omega_M\,T^*(w)^{(p-1)M}\Bigr]=\sum_{n\ge0}\sum_R a(R,n)\,t^R\,\Omega_{n+d(R)}\,w^{(p-1)n}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $T^*(w)=w(1+\sum_{j\ge1}\vartheta_j)$ with
$\vartheta_j=s_jw^{p^{-j}-1}$.  Then
$T^*(w)^{(p-1)M}=w^{(p-1)M}\sum_A\binom{(p-1)M}{|A|}\binom{|A|}{A}\vartheta^A$.
The $t_j$-exponent of $\vartheta^A$ is $A_j/p^j$, which is integral iff
$A_j=p^jr_j$.  In that case $\vartheta^A=t^Rw^{-(p-1)d(R)}$.  Put
$n=M-d(R)$.  Terms with $n<0$ vanish, because then
$(p-1)M<N(R)-|R|\le N(R)$.
\end{proof}

On $\Dperf$ the total operation has fractional $t$-exponents, since
$\Lambda_t(y^{1/p})=\Lambda_t(y)^{1/p}$.  For example, for $p=2$, $k=2$,
$\Lambda_t(c_{2,1}^{1/2})$ contains $t_1^{1/2}c_{2,0}^{1/2}$.  The operation $P^R$
is the coefficient of the integral monomial $t^R$.  So Theorem~\ref{thm:C}
says exactly that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F2}
  \pi\bigl[\Lambda_t\,\Omega(w)\bigr]=\pi\bigl[\Omega(T^*w)\bigr],\qquad \Omega(w)=\frac{w}{\psi(w)} .
\end{equation}
In degrees where $\Omega_n\in\Dk$ (in particular for $k=1$, and at the generator
degrees $n_j$), $\Lambda_t\Omega_n$ has integral exponents and the $\pi$ on the
left can be dropped.  Informally: up to terms with fractional $t$-exponents, the total Steenrod
operation acts on the Peterson series through the adjoint $T^*$.  The
mechanism is the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adj}
  S\bigl(x\,T^*(w)\bigr)=S\bigl(T(x)\,w\bigr)+B(x,w),\qquad
  B(x,w)=\sum_{j\ge1}\sum_{a<j}x^{p^a}(t_jw)^{p^{a-j}},
\end{equation}
obtained by splitting $\sum_{a,j}x^{p^a}(t_jw)^{p^{a-j}}$ according to whether
$a\ge j$.  Every monomial of $B$ has $t$-exponent $p^{-c}\varepsilon_j$ with
$c\ge1$, where $\varepsilon_j$ is the $j$-th unit vector.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:k1}
Theorem~\ref{thm:C} holds for $k=1$.  Hence the algorithm
of Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} is correct for $D_1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $k=1$, $\Omega_n=\wt_n$, since both equal
$(-1)^n\binom{-(p-1)n}{n}x^{(p-1)n}$.  By
Proposition~\ref{prop:monomials}, $\Omega(w)=1-S(xw)^{p-1}$.  Therefore
$\Lambda_t\Omega(w)=1-S(T(x)w)^{p-1}$ and $\Omega(T^*w)=1-S(xT^*w)^{p-1}$.
By~\eqref{eq:adj},
\[
  S(xT^*w)^e=\sum_{i=0}^e\binom ei S(T(x)w)^{e-i}B^i .
\]
For $1\le i\le e\le p-1$, every monomial of $B^i$ has $t$-exponent vector
equal to a sum of $i$ vectors of the form $p^{-c}\varepsilon_j$ with $c\ge1$.
Each coordinate of such a sum is either $0$ or lies in $(0,(p-1)/p]$, and at
least one coordinate is nonzero, so the sum is not integral.  Hence
$\pi(B^i)=0$, and since $S(T(x)w)$ is integral,
$\pi(S(xT^*w)^e)=S(T(x)w)^e$.  So
$\pi[\Omega(T^*w)]=\Lambda_t\Omega(w)$.  Now compare coefficients using
Lemma~\ref{lem:G}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The general case at the generators}
For $k\ge2$ the argument of Theorem~\ref{thm:k1}, applied to
$\prod_l(1-S(x_lT^*w)^{p-1})$, fails.  Fractional pieces coming from
\emph{different} factors can combine into integral monomials.  These cross
terms do not vanish in general; they are exactly why $(\ast)$ fails for
non-invariant $\wt_n$ (for example $p=2$, $k=2$, $n=1$, $R=(1)$).  At the
generators we can nevertheless prove the formula.  The first step reduces it to
a single identity.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:reduction}
Put
\[
  F_t(X)=\coef{w^{p^k}}\;\pi\bigl[\Omega(T^*w)\bigr]\,S(Xw)
  =\sum_{j=0}^k\Bigl(\sum_R a(R,n_j)\,t^R\,\Omega_{n_j+d(R)}\Bigr)X^{p^{k-j}} .
\]
Then~\eqref{eq:gen} holds for all $j$ and $R$ if and only if
$F_t\bigl(T(x_1)\bigr)=0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The two expressions for $F_t$ agree by Lemma~\ref{lem:G}.  For $j=0$ we have
$a(R,0)=\binom{N(R)-|R|}{N(R)}=0$ unless $R=0$, so $F_t$ is additive and monic
of degree $p^k$, with coefficients in $\Dperf[t]$.  It is symmetric in the
$x_i$.  So if $F_t(T(x_1))=0$, then $F_t$ vanishes on $T(V)$, and by (D2)
$F_t=f_{T(V)}=\Lambda_tf_V$ by~\eqref{eq:Lf}.  Comparing coefficients
gives~\eqref{eq:gen}.  The converse is the same computation read backwards.
\end{proof}

The rest of this subsection proves $F_t(T(x_1))=0$.  We need three tools: a
factorisation of the Peterson series, a bound on its support, and an explicit
formula for $F_t(T(X))$.  Throughout, $\alpha(m)$ denotes the base-$p$ digit
sum of $m\ge0$.  Recall that $\alpha(a+b)\le\alpha(a)+\alpha(b)$, with
equality iff the addition has no carries.  By Lucas' theorem, a multinomial
coefficient $\binom{a_1+\dots+a_m}{a_1,\dots,a_m}$ is nonzero mod $p$ iff the
addition $a_1+\dots+a_m$ has no carries.  Write
$g(z)=1-S(z)^{p-1}=z/S(z)$ and $\gamma_m=\coef{z^m}g(z)$.  By
Proposition~\ref{prop:monomials}, $\gamma_m\ne0$ only if $m=0$ or
$\alpha(m)=p-1$.

\emph{Factorisation.}  Let $0\ne v\in V$, $f_v(X)=X^p-v^{p-1}X$ and
$U=f_v(V)^{1/p}$, an $\F$-space of rank $k-1$.  Put $\psi_v(w)=S(vw)/v$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:factor}
$\Omega_V(w)=g(vw)\cdot\Omega_U\bigl(\psi_v(w)\bigr)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By (D2), $f_V=f_{f_v(V)}\circ f_v$.  Write additive series as twisted
polynomials in the Frobenius $F$, with adjoint $(aF^i)^*=F^{-i}a$ and
$(AB)^*=B^*A^*$.  Then $\varphi_V=f_V^*F^k$, and
\[
  \varphi_V=f_v^*\,f_{f_v(V)}^*\,F^k=(f_v^*F)\circ\bigl(F^{-1}(f_{f_v(V)}^*F^{k-1})F\bigr)
  =\varphi_{\langle v\rangle}\circ\varphi_U ,
\]
because conjugating by $F$ takes $p$-th roots of the coefficients.  Hence
$\psi_V=\psi_U\circ\psi_{\langle v\rangle}$ and
$w/\psi_V(w)=\frac{w}{\psi_{\langle v\rangle}(w)}\cdot
\frac{\psi_{\langle v\rangle}(w)}{\psi_U(\psi_{\langle v\rangle}(w))}$.  For rank
one, $\varphi_{\langle v\rangle}(u)=u-v^{p-1}u^p$, so
$\psi_{\langle v\rangle}=\psi_v$ and $w/\psi_v(w)=vw/S(vw)=g(vw)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Support]\label{lem:support}
Let $\eta(w)=\sum_{a\ge0}e_aw^{p^a}$ be any additive series.  Then
$\coef{w^E}\,\Omega_V(\eta(w))=0$ unless $\alpha(E)\le k(p-1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $k$; for $k=0$, $\Omega_V=1$.  By Lemma~\ref{lem:factor},
$\Omega_V(\eta(w))=g(v\eta(w))\,\Omega_U(\eta'(w))$ with $\eta'=\psi_v\circ\eta$
additive.  The first factor is $1-S(v\eta(w))^{p-1}$, and $S(v\eta(w))$ is
additive in $w$.  So the first factor is supported in exponents that are $0$ or
a sum of $p-1$ powers of $p$, i.e.\ of digit sum $\le p-1$.  By induction the
second factor is supported in digit sum $\le(k-1)(p-1)$.  Now use
subadditivity of $\alpha$.
\end{proof}

For $D_0\ge1$ put
\[
  F^{D_0}_t(X)=\coef{w^{D_0}}\;\pi\bigl[\Omega(T^*w)\bigr]\,S(Xw),
\]
so that $F_t=F^{p^k}_t$.  This is an additive polynomial in $X$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:FT}
Fix $D_0\ge1$.  For $R$ put $D_R=D_0+(p-1)d(R)$, and write $\coef{y^{pR}}$ for the coefficient
of $\prod_jy_j^{p^jr_j}$.  Put
\[
  b_R=\coef{y^{pR}}\bigl(1+\textstyle\sum_jy_j\bigr)^{D_R},\qquad
  \delta_{R,e}=\coef{y^{pR}}\Bigl(\sum_{j>e}y_j^{p^e}\Bigr)\bigl(1+\textstyle\sum_jy_j\bigr)^{D_R-p^e}.
\]
Then
\[
  F^{D_0}_t\bigl(T(X)\bigr)=\sum_Rt^R\coef{w^{D_R}}\,\Omega(w)\,B_R(Xw),\qquad
  B_R(z)=b_R\,S(z)-\sum_{e\ge0}\delta_{R,e}\,z^{p^e}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $S(T(X)w)$ has integral $t$-exponents,
$F^{D_0}_t(T(X))=\pi\coef{w^{D_0}}\Omega(T^*w)\,S(T(X)w)$.
By~\eqref{eq:adj}, $S(T(X)w)=S(XT^*w)-B(X,w)$.  The first part is
$\pi\coef{w^{D_0}}H(T^*w)$ with $H(w)=\Omega(w)S(Xw)$.  Expanding as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:G} gives $\sum_Rt^Rb_R\coef{w^{D_R}}H(w)$.  For the second
part, take a monomial $X^{p^a}(t_jw)^{p^{a-j}}$ of $B$ (so $a<j$) times the
term $\Omega_M w^{(p-1)M}\binom{(p-1)M}{|A|}\binom{|A|}{A}\vartheta^A$ of
$\Omega(T^*w)$.  Its $t$-exponents are integral iff $A_i=p^ir_i$ for $i\ne j$
and $A_j=p^jr_j-p^a$ with $r_j\ge1$.  Then its $w$-exponent is
$(p-1)M-(p-1)d(R)+p^a$, which equals $D_0$ iff $(p-1)M=D_R-p^a$.  Summing the
coefficients over $j>a$ gives $\delta_{R,a}$.  So the second part is
$-\sum_Rt^R\sum_a\delta_{R,a}X^{p^a}\Omega_{(D_R-p^a)/(p-1)}=-\sum_Rt^R\coef{w^{D_R}}\Omega(w)\sum_a\delta_{R,a}(Xw)^{p^a}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Digit sums]\label{lem:digits}
Let $D_0\ge1$, $R$ arbitrary, $e\ge0$ and $D=D_R=D_0+(p-1)d(R)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\coef{y^{pR}}(1+\sum_jy_j)^{D-1}\ne0$, then $\alpha(D-1)\ge\alpha(D_0-1)$.
\item If $\delta_{R,e}\ne0$, then $\alpha(D-p^e)\ge\alpha(D_0-1)+p-1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Recall $D-N(R)=D_0-|R|$.  (1) The coefficient is
$\binom{D-1}{N(R)}\binom{N(R)}{pR}$.  If it is nonzero, the parts $p^jr_j$ add
without carries, so $\alpha(N(R))=\sum_j\alpha(r_j)\ge\alpha(|R|)$.  Moreover
$D-1-N(R)=D_0-1-|R|\ge0$ and $N(R)+(D_0-1-|R|)$ has no carries.  Since
$\alpha(D_0-1)\le\alpha(D_0-1-|R|)+\alpha(|R|)$, we get
$\alpha(D-1)=\alpha(N(R))+\alpha(D_0-1-|R|)\ge\alpha(D_0-1)$.

(2) Some summand $\binom{D-p^e}{N(R)-p^e}\binom{N(R)-p^e}{pR-p^e\varepsilon_j}$
with $j>e$ and $r_j\ge1$ is nonzero.  The parts $p^ir_i$ ($i\ne j$) and
$p^jr_j-p^e=p^e\bigl(p^{j-e}(r_j-1)+p^{j-e}-1\bigr)$ add without carries, so
\[
  \alpha(N(R)-p^e)=\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha(r_i)+\alpha(r_j-1)+(j-e)(p-1).
\]
The binomial coefficient is nonzero, so $D-N(R)=D_0-|R|\ge0$ (with
$|R|\ge1$) and $\alpha(D-p^e)=\alpha(N(R)-p^e)+\alpha(D_0-|R|)$.  Moreover
$\alpha(D_0-|R|)\ge\alpha(D_0-1)-\alpha(|R|-1)$ and
$\alpha(|R|-1)\le\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha(r_i)+\alpha(r_j-1)$.  Hence
\[
  \alpha(D-p^e)\ge(j-e)(p-1)+\alpha(D_0-1)\ge\alpha(D_0-1)+p-1. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Vanishing]\label{prop:vanish}
If $\alpha(D_0-1)>(k-1)(p-1)$, then $F^{D_0}_t(T(v))=0$ for all $v\in V$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By additivity it suffices to take $v\ne0$.  By Lemma~\ref{lem:factor}, $\Omega_V(w)=g(vw)G(w)$ with
$G(w)=\Omega_U(\psi_v(w))$.  Since $g(z)S(z)=z$,
\[
  g(z)B_R(z)=\sum_EG_{R,E}\,z^E,\qquad
  G_{R,E}=b_R\,[E=1]-\sum_{e\ge0}\delta_{R,e}\,\gamma_{E-p^e}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:FT}, the coefficient of $t^R$ in $F^{D_0}_t(T(v))$ is
$\sum_EG_{R,E}\,v^E\coef{w^{D_R-E}}G(w)$.  We show that every term vanishes.
Suppose $\coef{w^{D_R-E}}G\ne0$.  By Lemma~\ref{lem:support} (rank $k-1$,
$\eta=\psi_v$), $\alpha(D_R-E)\le(k-1)(p-1)$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $E=1$, then
  $G_{R,1}=b_R-\delta_{R,0}=\coef{y^{pR}}(1+\sum y_j)^{D_R-1}$.  If this were
  nonzero, Lemma~\ref{lem:digits}(1) would give
  $\alpha(D_R-1)\ge\alpha(D_0-1)>(k-1)(p-1)$.  This is a contradiction.
\item If $E>1$, a nonzero summand $\delta_{R,e}\gamma_{E-p^e}$ has
  $\alpha(E-p^e)\le p-1$.  Hence
  $\alpha(D_R-p^e)\le\alpha(D_R-E)+\alpha(E-p^e)\le k(p-1)$.  But
  Lemma~\ref{lem:digits}(2) gives
  $\alpha(D_R-p^e)\ge\alpha(D_0-1)+p-1>k(p-1)$.
\end{itemize}
So $G_{R,E}=0$ whenever $\coef{w^{D_R-E}}G\ne0$, and $F^{D_0}_t(T(v))=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Action on the generators]\label{thm:gen}
Formula~\eqref{eq:gen} holds for all $k$, $j$ and $R$:
\[
  P^R c_{k,k-j}=a(R,n_j)\,\Omega_{n_j+d(R)} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take $D_0=p^k$, for which $\alpha(p^k-1)=k(p-1)>(k-1)(p-1)$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:vanish} gives $F_t(T(x_1))=0$; now apply
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:algo}
The algorithm of Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} computes the correct
$\A$-action on $\Dk$ for all $p$ and $k$.  Moreover, if $a(R,n_j)\ne0$, then
$\wt_{n_j+d(R)}=\Omega_{n_j+d(R)}$.  This is the case of Theorem~\ref{conj:B}
that the algorithm uses.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first claim follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:gen} and Section~\ref{sec:whole}.
For the second, run the same proof with the polynomial family $\wt_n$ in
place of $\Omega_n$.  Lemmas~\ref{lem:G} and~\ref{lem:FT} hold for any family.
The factorisation is $\wt(w)=g(x_1w)\prod_{l\ge2}g(x_lw)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:monomials}),
and the second factor is supported in digit sum $\le(k-1)(p-1)$.  So
$P^Rc_{k,k-j}=a(R,n_j)\,\wt_{n_j+d(R)}$ as well.
\end{proof}

For single powers $P^i$, Theorem~\ref{thm:gen} recovers the known formulas of
Hung~\cite{Hung91} ($p=2$) and Hung--Minh~\cite{HungMinh} (odd $p$; see
also~\cite[Thm.~3.4]{Sum21}).  We compared these with the code's output for all
$s$ and $i$ for $p=2$, $k=3,4$; $p=3$, $k=2,3$; and $p=5,7$, $k=2$, using
$c_{k,s}=(-1)^{k-s}Q_{k,s}$.  All 298 values agree.

\subsection{All degrees}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:all}
Theorem~\ref{thm:C} holds: $P^R\Omega_n=a(R,n)\,\Omega_{n+d(R)}$ for all $k$, $n$
and $R$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $G=\pi[\Omega_V(T^*w)]$ and $G'=\pi[\Lambda_t\Omega_V(w)]=\pi[\Omega_{T(V)}(w)]$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:G} we must show $G=G'$.  Let $\Delta=G'-G=\sum_n\Delta_nw^{(p-1)n}$.
Then $\Delta_0=0$, and every $\Delta_n$ has integral $t$-exponents.

\emph{Step 1: a family of linear conditions.}  Let $\alpha(D_0-1)>(k-1)(p-1)$ and
$u\in T(V)$.  By Proposition~\ref{prop:vanish},
$\coef{w^{D_0}}G(w)S(uw)=F^{D_0}_t(u)=0$.  Lemmas~\ref{lem:factor}
and~\ref{lem:support} hold for the rank-$k$ space $T(V)$ as well.  So
$\Omega_{T(V)}(w)S(uw)=uw\,\Omega_{U'}(\psi_u(w))$ is supported in exponents $E$
with $\alpha(E-1)\le(k-1)(p-1)$.  Since $S(uw)$ is integral, the same holds for
$G'(w)S(uw)$.  Hence the additive polynomial
\[
  L_{D_0}(X)=\coef{w^{D_0}}\Delta(w)S(Xw)=\sum_{e\ge0}\Delta_{(D_0-p^e)/(p-1)}X^{p^e}
\]
vanishes on $T(V)$.  Divide $L_{D_0}$ by the monic additive polynomial $f_{T(V)}$:
$L_{D_0}=M\circ f_{T(V)}+\mathrm{Rem}$, where $\mathrm{Rem}$ has degree $<p^k$.
The usual division algorithm works for additive polynomials over any ring of
characteristic $p$.  Since $\mathrm{Rem}$ vanishes on the $p^k$ elements of
$T(V)$, $\mathrm{Rem}=0$.

\emph{Step 2: a recursion.}  Let $C=\{n\ge1:p^{k-1}\mid(p-1)n\}$.  For $n\in C$
put $D_0=(p-1)n+p^{k-1}$.  The low $k-1$ digits of $(p-1)n$ vanish, so
$\alpha(D_0-1)=\alpha((p-1)n)+(k-1)(p-1)>(k-1)(p-1)$.  In
$L_{D_0}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the term $e=k-1$ is $\Delta_nX^{p^{k-1}}$;
\item the terms $e<k-1$ are $X^{p^e}$ with $e<k-1$, and are already reduced;
\item the terms $e\ge k$ have indices
  $n_e=n-(p^e-p^{k-1})/(p-1)<n$.  Here $(p-1)n_e\equiv0\bmod p^{k-1}$, so
  $n_e\in C\cup\{0\}$, or the term is absent.
\end{itemize}
Reducing $X^{p^e}$ ($e\ge k$) with $X^{p^k}\equiv-\sum_{i<k}c_i(T(V))X^{p^i}$, the
coefficient of $X^{p^{k-1}}$ in $\mathrm{Rem}$ is $\Delta_n+\sum_{e\ge k}\rho_e\Delta_{n_e}$
for certain $\rho_e$.  It vanishes by Step 1.  By induction on $n\in C$, starting
from $\Delta_0=0$, we get $\Delta_n=0$ for all $n\in C$.

\emph{Step 3: integral degrees.}  Let $N=p^km$ with $m\ge1$; then $N\in C$.
Moreover $\Omega_N=\Omega_m^{p^k}$: raise $(u/\varphi)^{(p-1)m}$ to the
$p^k$-th power in~\eqref{eq:Omega}.  This lies in $\Dk$, because
$q_j^{p^k}\in\Dk$.  So $\Lambda_t\Omega_N$ has integral $t$-exponents, and
Step 2 gives
\[
  \Lambda_t\Omega_N=\sum_St^S\,a(S,N)\,\Omega_{N+d(S)} .
\]

\emph{Step 4: descent.}  For arbitrary $m$,
$\Lambda_t\Omega_m=(\Lambda_t\Omega_{p^km})^{p^{-k}}$.  The coefficient of the
integral monomial $t^R$ comes from $S=p^kR$.  It is
$\bigl(a(p^kR,p^km)\,\Omega_{p^k(m+d(R))}\bigr)^{p^{-k}}=a(R,m)\,\Omega_{m+d(R)}$,
by Lucas' theorem and $\Omega_{p^kj}=\Omega_j^{p^k}$.
\end{proof}

For $k=1$, $C$ consists of all $n\ge1$, and Step 2 is
Theorem~\ref{thm:k1} again.

\subsection{The conjugate action}
On $\Dperf$ we let $\chi(P^R)$ act by the integral coefficients of
$\Lambda'_t=\Lambda_t^{-1}$, as for $P^R$.

The proof reduces the conjugate formula to the ordinary one, using the
antipode identity and a vanishing statement for binomial coefficients.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:conj}
Suppose that, for all $n\ge0$ and $R\ne0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:antipode}
  \sum_{R'+R''=R}b(R',\,n+d(R''))\;a(R'',\,n)=0 .
\end{equation}
Then Theorem~\ref{thm:conjact} holds.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the same descent as in Step 4, it suffices to treat $N$ with
$\Omega_N\in\Dk$.  The element $\chi(P^R)$ is a linear combination of Milnor
basis elements $P^S$ with $d(S)=d(R)$.  So by Theorem~\ref{thm:all},
$\chi(P^R)\Omega_N$ is a scalar multiple of $\Omega_{N+d(R)}$.  We show by
induction on $|R|$ that the scalar is $b(R,N)$, for all such $N$.  Apply
$\sum_{R'+R''=R}\chi(P^{R'})P^{R''}=0$ (the antipode identity, $R\ne0$) to
$\Omega_N$.  If $a(R'',N)\ne0$, then $\Omega_{N+d(R'')}=P^{R''}\Omega_N/a(R'',N)$
lies in $\Dk$, so for $R''\ne0$ the induction hypothesis applies to it.  Thus
\[
  \chi(P^R)\Omega_N=-\sum_{R''\ne0}b(R',N+d(R''))\,a(R'',N)\,\Omega_{N+d(R)}
  =b(R,N)\,\Omega_{N+d(R)}
\]
by~\eqref{eq:antipode}, since $a(0,\cdot)=1$.
\end{proof}

To prove~\eqref{eq:antipode} we need a projection on power series.  Let
$y_1,\dots,y_J$ be variables, $Y=1+\sum_iy_i$, and fix levels
$\lambda=(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_J)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^J$.  Let $\Pi_\lambda$
keep the monomials $y^E$ with $p^{\lambda_i}\mid E_i$ for all $i$, and put
$\|E\|_\lambda=\sum_iE_i/p^{\lambda_i}$ for such $E$.  For $m\in\mathbb{Z}$, $Y^m$
is a power series, a polynomial if $m\ge0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:levels}
Let $E\ne0$ with $p^{\lambda_i}\mid E_i$, and let $\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Z}$ with
$\mu+\nu=|E|-\delta$, where $1\le\delta\le\|E\|_\lambda$.  Then
\[
  \coef{y^E}\,\Pi_\lambda(Y^\mu)\,\Pi_\lambda(Y^\nu)=0 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $m=\sum_am_ap^a$ in base $p$ ($p$-adically if $m<0$).  By Lucas,
$Y^m=\prod_a(1+\sum_iy_i^{p^a})^{m_a}$.  In this product the exponent of $y_i$ is
$\sum_ap^ak_{a,i}$ with digits $k_{a,i}\le m_a<p$, so it is divisible by
$p^{\lambda_i}$ iff $k_{a,i}=0$ for $a<\lambda_i$.  Hence
\[
  \Pi_\lambda(Y^m)=\prod_{a\ge0}\Bigl(1+\sum_{i:\lambda_i\le a}y_i^{p^a}\Bigr)^{m_a}
  =\Bigl(1+\sum_{i\in Z}y_i\Bigr)^{m_0}\cdot\Pi_{\lambda^-}\bigl(Y^{\lfloor m/p\rfloor}\bigr)^p,
\]
where $Z=\{i:\lambda_i=0\}$, $\lambda^-_i=\max(\lambda_i-1,0)$ and
$\lfloor m/p\rfloor=(m-m_0)/p$.  We use induction on
$\sum_{i\notin Z,\,E_i\ne0}\lambda_i$.

If $E_i=0$ for all $i\notin Z$, set those $y_i=0$.  Then
$\Pi_\lambda(Y^m)$ becomes $(1+\sum_{i\in Z}y_i)^m$, and the coefficient is
$\binom{|E|-\delta}{|E|}\binom{|E|}{E}=0$, since $0\le|E|-\delta<|E|$.

Otherwise let $s=\mu_0+\nu_0\le2p-2$, $\mu'=\lfloor\mu/p\rfloor$,
$\nu'=\lfloor\nu/p\rfloor$.  Then the coefficient is a sum, over splittings
$E_i=e_i+pe'_i$ ($i\in Z$), of $\coef{y^e}(1+\sum_{i\in Z}y_i)^s$ times
$\coef{y^{E''}}\Pi_{\lambda^-}(Y^{\mu'})\Pi_{\lambda^-}(Y^{\nu'})$.  Here
$E''_i=E_i/p$ for $i\notin Z$ and $E''_i=e'_i$ for $i\in Z$.  Only splittings
with $|e|\le s$ contribute.  We check the hypotheses for $(\lambda^-,E'',\mu',\nu')$.
We have $|E|=p|E''|+|e|$, so $\mu'+\nu'=(\mu+\nu-s)/p=|E''|-\delta''$ with
$\delta''=(\delta+s-|e|)/p$, an integer.  Since $\delta\ge1$ and $|e|\le s$,
$\delta''\ge1$.  Let $c=\sum_{i\notin Z}E_i/p^{\lambda_i}\ge1$, so that
$\|E\|_\lambda=c+|e|+p|e'|$ and $\|E''\|_{\lambda^-}=c+|e'|$.  Then
$\delta''\le(c+s)/p+|e'|<c+1+|e'|$, using $s\le2p-2<(p-1)c+p$.  So
$\delta''\le\|E''\|_{\lambda^-}$.  If $E''=0$, then $|E|=|e|\le s$ and $c=0$,
which is excluded.  So the induction hypothesis applies, and every term
vanishes.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:conjall}
Identity~\eqref{eq:antipode} holds.  Hence Theorem~\ref{thm:conjact} holds.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\rho_a(t)$ and $\rho_b(t)$ be the operators on formal symbols
$(E_n)_{n\ge0}$ given by $E_n\mapsto\sum_Rt^Ra(R,n)E_{n+d(R)}$ and
$E_n\mapsto\sum_Rt^Rb(R,n)E_{n+d(R)}$.  Identity~\eqref{eq:antipode} says
$\rho_b\rho_a=1$.  Both operators are the identity plus terms of positive
$t$-degree, so this is equivalent to $\rho_a\rho_b=1$, i.e.\ to
\[
  \sum_{R_1+R_2=R}b(R_1,n)\,a(R_2,n+d(R_1))=0\qquad(R\ne0).
\]
Now $b(R_1,n)=\coef{y^{pR_1}}Y^{-(p-1)n}$, where
$y^{pR}=\prod_jy_j^{p^jr_j}$.  Similarly
$a(R_2,n+d(R_1))=\coef{y^{pR_2}}Y^{(p-1)n+N(R)-|R|}$, because
$(p-1)(n+d(R_1)+d(R_2))=(p-1)n+N(R)-|R|$.  So the sum is
$\coef{y^{pR}}\Pi_\lambda(Y^\mu)\Pi_\lambda(Y^\nu)$ with $\lambda_j=j$,
$\mu=-(p-1)n$, $\nu=(p-1)n+N(R)-|R|$.  Here $E=pR$ has $|E|=N(R)$ and
$\|E\|_\lambda=|R|$, and $\mu+\nu=|E|-|R|$.  Lemma~\ref{lem:levels} with
$\delta=|R|$ shows that it vanishes.  Now apply Proposition~\ref{prop:conj}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Two-sided Peterson elements and Theorem~\ref{conj:B}}\label{sec:arnon}
For $p=2$, Arnon~\cite{Arnon} replaces the one-sided series $S$ by a
two-sided one.  This subsection does the same for all $p$.  The result is a
family $\Theta_n$ on which the total operation acts \emph{exactly} through the
adjoint $T^*$, and which coincides with the $\Omega_n$.

We work in the perfect closure $H^{\mathrm{perf}}$ of $H$, whose
$\GL_k$-invariants are $\Dperf$.  Extend the digit sum $\alpha$ and the
$p$-adic valuation $\nu=\nu_p$ to $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]_{\ge0}$.  Call
$a\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p]_{\ge0}$ \emph{two-sided admissible} if $a=0$ or
$\alpha(a)=p-1$, and write $a=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\varepsilon_rp^r$.  For an
integer $n\ge1$ put
\[
  \sigma_p(n)=\frac{\alpha((p-1)n)}{p-1}+\nu_p(n).
\]
For $p=2$ this is $\alpha(n)+\nu(n)$.

\begin{definition}
Let $\widehat S(z)=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}z^{p^r}$, and define $\Theta_n$ for
$n\in\mathbb{N}[\frac1p]$ by
\[
  \Theta(w)=\prod_{i=1}^k\bigl(1-\widehat S(x_iw)^{p-1}\bigr)
  =\sum_{n}\Theta_n\,w^{(p-1)n}.
\]
As in Proposition~\ref{prop:monomials}(2),
\[
  \Theta_n=\sum_{\substack{a_1+\dots+a_k=(p-1)n\\ a_i\ \text{two-sided admissible}}}
  \ \prod_{i=1}^k\frac{1}{\varepsilon(a_i)!}\;x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_k^{a_k}.
\]
\end{definition}

Different tuples $(a_i)$ give different monomials, and all coefficients are
nonzero.  So $\Theta_n$ contains a given monomial if and only if its exponent
vector is such a tuple.  For $p=2$, $\Theta_n$ is Arnon's element
${}_k\omega_n$~\cite[Def.~3.2]{Arnon}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:twosupport}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $m=a_1+\dots+a_k>0$ with all $a_i$ two-sided admissible.  Then every
  digit position $r$ that occurs in some $a_i$ satisfies
  $r\ge\nu(m)-k+\alpha(m)/(p-1)$.  In particular each $\Theta_n$ is a finite
  sum.
\item For an integer $n\ge1$, $\Theta_n$ has integral exponents if and only if
  $\sigma_p(n)\ge k$.  In that case $\Theta_n=\wt_n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) Let $r$ be the lowest digit position occurring in the $a_i$.  If
$r\ge\nu(m)$, the claim holds because $\alpha(m)\le\sum\alpha(a_i)\le k(p-1)$.
Otherwise, add the $a_i$ column by column.  Column $r$ of the sum is nonzero,
but the digits of $m$ in positions $r,\dots,\nu(m)-1$ vanish.  So there is a
carry out of each of these $\nu(m)-r$ columns.  Every unit carried lowers the
digit sum by $p-1$, so
\[
  k(p-1)\ge\sum_i\alpha(a_i)\ge\alpha(m)+(p-1)(\nu(m)-r).
\]
Finiteness follows, since the digit positions are also bounded above by
$\log_pm$.

(2) Put $m=(p-1)n$, so that $\nu(m)=\nu(n)$.  If $\sigma_p(n)\ge k$, then (1)
gives $r\ge\sigma_p(n)-k\ge0$.  So all exponents are integral, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:monomials}(2) shows $\Theta_n=\wt_n$.

Conversely, let $\sigma_p(n)<k$.  Write the lowest nonzero digit unit of $m$ as
\[
  p^{\nu}=(p-1)(p^{\nu-1}+\dots+p^0)+p\cdot p^{-1}.
\]
This turns the $\alpha(m)$ digit units of $m$ into
$\alpha(m)+(\nu+1)(p-1)=(\sigma_p(n)+1)(p-1)$ units, one of which sits in
position $-1$.  Split them into $\sigma_p(n)+1\le k$ groups of $p-1$ units
each.  Each group is a two-sided admissible number, because no position can
hold more than $p-1$ units of one group.  So $\Theta_n$ contains a monomial
with a non-integral exponent.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:twoaction}
$\Lambda_t\Theta(w)=\Theta(T^*w)$.  Consequently, for all integers $n\ge0$
and all $R$,
\[
  P^R\,\Theta_n=a(R,n)\,\Theta_{n+d(R)} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The series $\widehat S$ is additive.  It also satisfies
$\widehat S(y^{p^c})=\widehat S(y)$ for every $y$ and every $c\in\mathbb{Z}$,
because the substitution only shifts the summation index.  The operation
$\Lambda_t$ is a ring map on the perfect closure with
$\Lambda_t(x^{p^r})=T(x)^{p^r}$.  Hence
\[
  \Lambda_t\widehat S(xw)=\widehat S(T(x)w)=\sum_j\widehat S(t_jx^{p^j}w)
  =\sum_j\widehat S\bigl(x\,(t_jw)^{p^{-j}}\bigr)=\widehat S\bigl(x\,T^*(w)\bigr).
\]
Taking the product over $i$ gives $\Lambda_t\Theta(w)=\Theta(T^*w)$.

Now apply $\pi$ and compare the coefficients of $w^{(p-1)n}$ with $n$
integral.  On the left this is $\sum_Rt^RP^R\Theta_n$.  On the right, use the
expansion in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:G}.  For $M\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p]$ write
$(1+X)^{(p-1)M}=\bigl((1+X)^{(p-1)Mp^e}\bigr)^{p^{-e}}$.  The terms with
integral $t$-exponents again have $w$-exponent $(p-1)(M-d(R))$.  So the
$\Theta_M$ with $M\notin\mathbb{Z}$ contribute nothing to integral powers.
Lemma~\ref{lem:G}, applied to the family $(\Theta_M)_{M\in\mathbb{N}}$, gives
the claim.
\end{proof}

This is the identity behind~\eqref{eq:F2}.  For the one-sided series $S$ it
only holds up to the error term $B$ of~\eqref{eq:adj}.  The two-sided series
absorbs $B$, because $\widehat S(y^{p^c})=\widehat S(y)$ also holds for
$c<0$.  For $p=2$, Theorem~\ref{thm:twoaction} contains Arnon's
formula $Sq^t\,{}_k\omega_n=\binom{n+t}{2t}{}_k\omega_{n+t}$ for integral
$n,t$~\cite[Thm.~3.9]{Arnon}.  He also proves it for half-integral $n,t$, by a
counting argument.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:arnon}
$\Omega_n=\Theta_n$ for all $p$, $k$ and all integers $n\ge0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $n$.  Both sides are $1$ for $n=0$.

\emph{Frobenius.}  Put $i=(p-1)m$.  Then $a((i),m)=\binom{p(p-1)m}{p(p-1)m}=1$,
and $P^i$ (or $Sq^i$ for $p=2$) raises elements of the degree of $\Omega_m$ to
the $p$-th power.  So Theorem~\ref{thm:all} gives $\Omega_{pm}=\Omega_m^p$.
Also $\Theta_{pm}=\Theta_m^p$, because $\Theta(w)^p=\Theta(w)$ by
$\widehat S(y)^p=\widehat S(y)$.

\emph{The case $n=1$.}  We have
$\Omega_1^{p^{k-1}}=\Omega_{p^{k-1}}=c_{k,k-1}$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:Aomega},
since $n_1=p^{k-1}$.  Also $\sigma_p(p^{k-1})=1+(k-1)=k$.  So
Lemma~\ref{lem:twosupport} and Theorem~\ref{thm:A} give
$\Theta_1^{p^{k-1}}=\Theta_{p^{k-1}}=\wt_{p^{k-1}}=c_{k,k-1}$.  Frobenius is
injective on $H^{\mathrm{perf}}$, so $\Omega_1=\Theta_1$.

\emph{The case $n\ge2$.}  Then $(p-1)n\ge p$, so $(p-1)n$ has a nonzero digit
$d$ in some position $e\ge1$.  Put $t=p^{e-1}$.  By Lucas' theorem,
$a((t),n-t)=\binom{(p-1)n}{p^e}=d\ne0$, and $t<n$.  By
Theorems~\ref{thm:all} and~\ref{thm:twoaction},
\[
  \Omega_n=d^{-1}P^t\,\Omega_{n-t}=d^{-1}P^t\,\Theta_{n-t}=\Theta_n. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:transvection}
Let $k\ge2$, $n\ge1$, and let $\tau$ be the substitution $x_1\mapsto x_1+x_2$.
The part of $\tau(\wt_n)-\wt_n$ of $x_1$-degree $0$ is the coefficient of
$w^{(p-1)n}$ in
\[
  -x_2w\,S(x_2w)^{p-2}\prod_{l=3}^k\bigl(1-S(x_lw)^{p-1}\bigr).
\]
It is nonzero if and only if $\sigma_p(n)<k$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $G(z)=1-S(z)^{p-1}=\sum_ag(a)z^a$, so that
$\wt(w)=\prod_lG(x_lw)$.  For an admissible $a$, the coefficient of $x_1^0$ in
$(x_1+x_2)^a$ is $x_2^a$.  So the coefficient of $x_1^0x_2^bx_3^{a_3}\cdots$ in
$\tau(\wt_n)$ is $\sum_{a_1+a_2=b}g(a_1)g(a_2)\prod_{l\ge3}g(a_l)$.  In $\wt_n$
the same coefficient is $g(b)\prod_{l\ge3}g(a_l)$.  So the part of
$x_1$-degree $0$ of $\tau(\wt(w))-\wt(w)$ is
$\bigl(G^2-G\bigr)(x_2w)\prod_{l\ge3}G(x_lw)$.  By~\eqref{eq:S},
\[
  G^2-G=S^{2(p-1)}-S^{p-1}=S^{p-2}(S^p-S)=-zS^{p-2}.
\]

The expansion of $S^{p-2}$ has nonzero coefficients $(p-2)!/\varepsilon!$ and
distinct exponents.  These exponents are the $e\ge0$ with $\alpha(e)=p-2$.  So
the expression is nonzero if and only if, with $m=(p-1)n$,
\[
  m-1=e+a_3+\dots+a_k,\qquad \alpha(e)=p-2,\ a_l\ \text{admissible}.
\]
Now $\alpha(m-1)=\alpha(m)-1+\nu(m)(p-1)=(\sigma_p(n)-1)(p-1)+(p-2)$.  Since
the digit sum is subadditive, a decomposition with $j$ nonzero $a_l$ needs
$(p-2)+j(p-1)\ge\alpha(m-1)$, i.e.\ $j\ge\sigma_p(n)-1$.  Conversely, the
digit units of $m-1$ can be split into one group of $p-2$ units and
$\sigma_p(n)-1$ groups of $p-1$ units.  So a decomposition exists if and only if
$\sigma_p(n)-1\le k-2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:conjB2}
Theorem~\ref{conj:B} holds.  More precisely, for all $p$, $k$ and $n\ge1$ the
following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\wt_n$ is $\GL_k$-invariant;
\item $\Omega_n\in\Dk$;
\item $\sigma_p(n)\ge k$.
\end{enumerate}
When they hold, $\wt_n=\Omega_n$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:arnon} and Lemma~\ref{lem:twosupport}, $\Omega_n=\Theta_n$
lies in $H$ if and only if $\sigma_p(n)\ge k$, and then it equals $\wt_n$.
Since $\Omega_n\in\Dperf$, this proves (2)$\Leftrightarrow$(3) and
(3)$\Rightarrow$(1).  For $k=1$, (3) always holds.

Let $k\ge2$ and $\sigma_p(n)<k$.  Then $\tau(\wt_n)\ne\wt_n$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:transvection}, so $\wt_n$ is not invariant.
\end{proof}

For example, for $p=2$, $k=3$, the integers $n\le40$ with $\sigma_2(n)<3$ are
$1,2,3,5,9,17,33$.  For $p=3$, $k=2$, the integers with $\sigma_3(n)<2$ are
$1,2,5,14,41,\dots$, i.e.\ $2n=2$ or $2n=3^j+1$.  These are exactly the
non-invariant $\wt_n$ listed after Proposition~\ref{prop:monomials}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twoconj}
Let $U=T^{-1}$, so that $\Lambda'_t(x)=U(x)=\sum_ju_jx^{p^j}$ with
$u_j\in\F[t]$, and let $U^*(w)=\sum_j(u_jw)^{p^{-j}}$ be its adjoint.  Then
$U^*=(T^*)^{-1}$, and
\[
  \Lambda'_t\Theta(w)=\Theta(U^*w).
\]
For all integers $n\ge0$ and all $R$,
$\chi(P^R)\,\Theta_n=b(R,n)\,\Theta_{n+d(R)}$.  This statement, for all $k$,
is equivalent to the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gconj}
  \pi\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{U^*(w)}{w}\Bigr)^{(p-1)M}\Bigr]
  =\sum_R b\bigl(R,M-d(R)\bigr)\,t^R\,w^{-(p-1)d(R)}\qquad(M\in\mathbb{N}),
\end{equation}
which only involves the series $U^*$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The computation in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:twoaction} applies to any
ring map $x\mapsto\sum_ju_jx^{p^j}$.  It gives
$\Lambda'_t\widehat S(xw)=\widehat S(U(x)w)=\widehat S(xU^*(w))$, and hence
the displayed identity.  Taking adjoints reverses composition, so $U^*=(T^*)^{-1}$.
The formula for $\chi(P^R)\Theta_n$ follows from $\Theta_n=\Omega_n$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:arnon}) and Theorem~\ref{thm:conjall}.

For the equivalence, give $t_j$ degree $p^j-1$ and $w$ degree $1$.  Then
$T^*$, and hence $U^*$, is homogeneous of degree $1$.  So a monomial
$t^Rw^\beta$ of $U^*(w)^{(p-1)M}$ with integral $R$ has
$\beta=(p-1)(M-d(R))$; in particular non-integral $M$ only contribute to
non-integral powers of $w$.  The projection $\pi$ is linear over the $t$-free
elements $\Theta_M$.  Comparing coefficients of $t^Rw^{(p-1)n}$ in
$\pi[\Lambda'_t\Theta(w)]=\sum_M\Theta_M\,\pi[U^*(w)^{(p-1)M}]$ therefore
gives
\[
  \chi(P^R)\,\Theta_n=\coef{t^R}\Bigl(\frac{U^*(w)}{w}\Bigr)^{(p-1)M}w^{(p-1)d(R)}
  \cdot\Theta_M,\qquad M=n+d(R).
\]
Since $\Theta_M\ne0$ once $k(p-1)\ge\alpha((p-1)M)$, the formula for all $k$
is equivalent to~\eqref{eq:Gconj}.  Terms of~\eqref{eq:Gconj} with $M<d(R)$
vanish, because then $(p-1)(d(R)-M)<N(R)$, so the binomial factor of $b$ is
$\binom{(p-1)(d(R)-M)}{N(R)}=0$.
\end{proof}

Identity~\eqref{eq:Gconj} is the conjugate analogue of Lemma~\ref{lem:G}.
Unlike Lemma~\ref{lem:G}, it does not seem to have a direct proof.  For
$T^*(w)=w(1+\sum_j\vartheta_j)$ the integral $t$-exponents are read off
monomial by monomial.  The coefficients $u_j$ of $U^*$, by contrast, are the
images of Milnor's conjugates $\chi(\xi_j)$, and products of terms with
fractional $t$-exponents can have integral exponents.  Lagrange inversion
does not help either.  The exponents of $U^*$ lie in $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]$, so the
derivative it needs is not defined in characteristic~$p$.  Its
characteristic-$0$ form predicts an extra factor
$c/(c-N(R)+|R|)$, with $c=(p-1)M$.  This is already wrong for $p=3$,
$R=(1)$, $M=2$: it gives $1$ instead of $b((1),1)=2$.
So~\eqref{eq:Gconj} rests on the antipode identity~\eqref{eq:antipode} and
Lemma~\ref{lem:levels}, as Theorem~\ref{thm:conjall} does.  The two-sided
series makes the ordinary action formula immediate, but not the conjugate one.

\begin{remark}[Comparison with Arnon]\label{rem:twosided}
For $p=2$ the results of this subsection overlap with~\cite[\S3]{Arnon}.
Arnon proves that ${}_k\omega_n=\Theta_n$ is invariant, satisfies
${}_k\omega_{2n}={}_k\omega_n^2$ and has integral exponents if and only if
$\sigma_2(n)\ge k$~\cite[Prop.~3.4, 3.6]{Arnon}.  He also computes $Sq^t$ on it
(Theorem~3.9), and writes it as a polynomial in the Dickson invariants
(Theorem~3.11, the analogue of our decomposition~\eqref{eq:Omega}).

The identity $\widehat S^p=\widehat S$ makes $\Theta(w)$ stable under
Frobenius, which is the generating-function form of
$\Theta_{pn}=\Theta_n^p$.  The one-sided series instead satisfies
$S-S^p=z$, and the difference is the error term $B$ of~\eqref{eq:adj}.
\end{remark}

\section{The action on the whole algebra}\label{sec:whole}

The code computes $P^R(m)$ for a monomial $m$ through a \emph{coaction}: a list
of triples $(y,Q,R')$, standing for $y\otimes\xi^{R'}$ (and exterior
$\tau^Q$).  Then $P^R(m)$ is the sum of the $y$ with $R'=R$
(\code{\_act\_from\_coact} in \code{algebra\_base.py}).  The ingredients are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Generators.}  For $c_{k,k-j}$ the coaction is
  $\sum_R a(R,n_j)\,\Omega_{n_j+d(R)}\otimes\xi^R$, truncated to the $R$ that
  are dominated by the requested operation.
\item \emph{Powers.}  The function \code{\_factor\_key} writes an exponent in
  base $p$, $g^{e}=\prod_u (g^{p^u})^{\varepsilon_u}$ with $0\le\varepsilon_u<p$.
  The coaction of $g^{p^u}$ is that of $g$ with everything raised to the $p^u$-th
  power, since $P^R(y^{p^u})=(P^{R/p^u}y)^{p^u}$, or $0$ if $p^u\nmid R$.  For
  $\varepsilon_u>1$ a multinomial expansion is used (\code{\_multinomials}).
\item \emph{Products.}  Coactions of factors are multiplied
  (\code{\_coaction\_helper}).  This is the Cartan formula
  $P^R(yz)=\sum_{R'+R''=R}P^{R'}(y)P^{R''}(z)$, i.e.\ multiplicativity of
  $\Lambda_t$.
\item \emph{Negative powers.}  In the localised algebra (the elements
  \code{\_zetapower}, \code{\_xipower} involve $c_{k,0}^{-1}$), a negative
  exponent $-e$ is written as $-p^u+(p^u-e)$, with $p^u$ larger than every
  entry of $R$.  Then $c_{k,0}^{-p^u}$ is invariant under all relevant $P^{R'}$.
\item \emph{M\`ui generators.}  The exterior generators $\tau_n$ are handled by
  the coaction of Theorem~\ref{thm:mui} (\code{\_tau\_coaction}), using the
  Dickson part for the coefficients $\Lambda(\rho_{N,j})$; see
  Section~\ref{sec:mui}.
\end{enumerate}
Because $\Dk$ is a polynomial algebra, (1)--(3) determine the action
completely.  Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:gen} this proves that the $\Dk$
part of the code is correct for the ordinary action.  The conjugate action
(\code{left\_steenrod\_action\_milnor\_conj}) uses Theorem~\ref{thm:conjact} on
the Dickson generators, so its $\Dk$ part is correct too.  The code also offers an
independent route, \code{admissible\_action}, through admissible matrices, and
restriction maps $D_k\to D_{k'}$ for $k'<k$; these are not discussed here.

\section{The M\`ui generators}\label{sec:mui}

Let $p$ be odd, or $p=2$ with the generic Steenrod algebra.  Then
$H^*(BV_k)=E(y_1,\dots,y_k)\otimes H$ with $|y_i|=1$ and $\beta y_i=x_i$.  We
extend the total operation by odd parameters $e_0,e_1,\dots$ dual to the Milnor
primitives $Q_l$:
\[
  \Lambda(x)=T(x)=\sum_jt_jx^{p^j},\qquad
  \Lambda(y)=y+E(x),\quad E(X)=\sum_le_lX^{p^l}.
\]
This is Milnor's coaction $\lambda(y)=y\otimes1+\sum_lx^{p^l}\otimes\tau_l$ in
total form.

\emph{The generators.}  The Moore matrix $(x_i^{p^j})_{i,j<k}$ is invertible
over $\mathrm{Frac}(H)$.  So there are unique elements $\tau_0,\dots,\tau_{k-1}$,
linear in the $y_i$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:muidef}
  y_v=\tau(x_v):=\sum_{j<k}\tau_j\,x_v^{p^j}\qquad\text{for all }v\in V,
\end{equation}
where $y_v,x_v$ denote the classes corresponding to $v$.  By uniqueness the
$\tau_j$ are $\GL_k$-invariant, and $|\tau_j|=1-2p^j$.  Up to sign,
$\tau_j=M_{k,j}/L_k$ is M\`ui's invariant divided by the Moore determinant,
equivalently $R_{k,j}/Q_{k,0}$~\cite{Mui}.  These are the generators
\code{tau\{j\}} of \code{DicksonAlgebra(p,k)} (with \code{generic=True} if
$p=2$).  Write $\rho_{N,j}\in\Dk$ for the coefficients of the reduction
$X^{p^N}\equiv\sum_{j<k}\rho_{N,j}X^{p^j}\bmod f_V$
(\code{\_reduced\_frobenius}).  Also let $C=T^{-1}=\sum_mc_mX^{p^m}$, so that
$c_m=\chi(\xi_m)(t)$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:mui}
\[
  \Lambda(\tau_j)=\sum_{N\ge0}\Bigl[\sum_{i+m=N}\tau_i\,c_m^{p^i}
  +\sum_{l+m=N}e_l\,c_m^{p^l}\Bigr]\Lambda(\rho_{N,j}) .
\]
In particular $Q_l\tau_j=\rho_{l,j}$, which is $\delta_{lj}$ for $l<k$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply the ring homomorphism $\Lambda$ to~\eqref{eq:muidef}.  This gives
$\Lambda(\tau)(T(v))=y_v+E(x_v)=(\tau+E)(v)$ for $v\in V$, where $\Lambda(\tau)$
is the additive polynomial $\sum_j\Lambda(\tau_j)X^{p^j}$.  Consider the
additive series $(\tau+E)\circ C$, which converges $t$-adically.  It takes the
same values on $T(V)$ as $\Lambda(\tau)$.  Divide by the monic polynomial
$f_{T(V)}=\Lambda(f_V)$.  The remainder of $\Lambda(\tau)-(\tau+E)\circ C$ has
degree $<p^k$ and vanishes on the $p^k$ elements of $T(V)$, so it is zero.
Since $\Lambda(\tau)$ already has degree $<p^k$, it equals the remainder of
$(\tau+E)\circ C$.  Now
$(\tau+E)\circ C=\sum_N\bigl[\sum_{i+m=N}\tau_ic_m^{p^i}+\sum_{l+m=N}e_lc_m^{p^l}\bigr]X^{p^N}$.
The remainder of $X^{p^N}$ modulo $f_{T(V)}$ is
$\sum_j\Lambda(\rho_{N,j})X^{p^j}$, because $\rho_{N,j}$ is a polynomial in the
Dickson invariants.
\end{proof}

In coaction language, $t_j\leftrightarrow\xi_j$, $c_m\leftrightarrow\chi(\xi_m)$
and $e_l\leftrightarrow\tau_l$.  The coefficients $\Lambda(\rho_{N,j})$ are
computed by the Dickson part (Theorem~\ref{thm:all}).  The conjugate coaction is
obtained by applying $1\otimes\chi$.  In it $c_m\leftrightarrow\xi_m$ and
$e_l\leftrightarrow\chi(\tau_l)=-\sum_{j\le l}\chi(\xi_{l-j})^{p^j}\tau_j$.

\begin{remark}
Earlier versions of the code used the model
$\Delta\tau_n=\tau_n\otimes1+\sum_i\xi_{n-i}^{p^i}\otimes\tau_i$, with $\xi_j$ the
elements \code{\_xipower(j)} of $\Dk[c_{k,0}^{-1}]$.  It made reduced powers
kill the $\tau_n$.  This is not an $\A$-module.  For $p=2$, $k=3$, a direct
polynomial computation gives $P(8)(c_{3,1}/c_{3,0})=c_{3,0}\ne0$, whereas
$P(8)\xi_1=0$ in $\A_*$.  Consequently
$(P(8)Q_0)(\tau_1\tau_2)=0\ne P(8)(Q_0(\tau_1\tau_2))$ in that model.  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:mui}, reduced powers do not kill the $\tau_n$; for example
$P^1\tau_1=-\tau_0$.
\end{remark}

\emph{Verification.}  For $p=3$ ($k=1,2$), $p=5$ ($k=2$) and $p=2$ generic
($k=2$), the new code agrees with a direct computation of $P^r\tau_j$ in
$H^*(BV_k)$, where $\tau_j$ is written as an explicit linear form in the $y_i$
with rational coefficients.  The action passes the associativity tests, for
both the ordinary and the conjugate action, on the regions where the old model
failed.

\section{The dual Dickson algebras}\label{sec:dual}

The class \code{DicksonDualSteenrodAlgebra($p$,$k$)} implements
\[
  \A_*(k)=\F[\xi_1,\dots,\xi_k]\otimes E(\tau_0,\dots,\tau_{k-1})\subset\A_* ,
\]
with the generators placed in negative degrees ($|\xi_j|=-2(p^j-1)$,
$|\tau_j|=1-2p^j$).  The code prints the $\xi_j$ as \code{zeta[j]}.  Milnor's
coproduct $\Delta\xi_n=\sum_i\xi_{n-i}^{p^i}\otimes\xi_i$ only involves
indices $\le n$, and similarly for $\tau_n$.  So $\A_*(k)$ is a sub-bialgebra
of~$\A_*$.  It is an $\A$-module algebra via
$\theta\cdot y=\sum y_{(1)}\langle\theta,y_{(2)}\rangle$
(\code{\_coproduct\_gen} and \code{\_left\_steenrod\_coaction\_milnor\_gen}).

It is related to the Dickson algebras in two ways.

\emph{(a) The localised Dickson algebra.}  The algebra map
$\xi_i\mapsto c_{k,i}/c_{k,0}$ (\code{DicksonAlgebra.\_zetapower}) sends
$\A_*(k)$'s polynomial part into $\Dk[c_{k,0}^{-1}]$ and preserves degrees.
The code uses its conjugate (\code{\_xipower}) for the M\`ui generators, as
explained in Section~\ref{sec:whole}.

\emph{(b) Peterson elements are images of a canonical element of $\A_*$.}  Let
$\xi(w)=\sum_j\xi_jw^{p^j}$ and $\zeta(w)=\sum_j\chi(\xi_j)w^{p^j}$.  These
are compositional inverses of each other, because
$\sum_i\chi(\xi_i)\xi_{n-i}^{p^i}=0$ for $n\ge1$.  Let
$\mu\colon\A_*\to\Dperf$ be the algebra map with $\chi(\xi_j)\mapsto q_j$ for
$j\le k$ and $\chi(\xi_j)\mapsto0$ for $j>k$.  Then $\mu(\zeta)=\varphi$,
$\mu(\xi)=\psi$, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:lagrange}
\[
  \sum_n\Omega_n\,w^{(p-1)n}=\mu\Bigl(\frac{w}{\xi(w)}\Bigr)
  =\mu\Bigl(\sum_R(-1)^{|R|}\binom{|R|}{R}\xi^R\,w^{(p-1)d(R)}\Bigr).
\]
The roots of $\varphi$ form an $\F$-subspace $Y$ of dimension $k$, in degree
$-|x_1|$, of an algebraic closure.  Since $\varphi$ has linear coefficient $1$
and top coefficient $c_{k,0}$, we have $\varphi=f_Y/c_0(Y)$ and
$q_j=c_j(Y)/c_0(Y)$.  So, up to the conjugation $\chi$, $\mu$ is the map of (a)
for the ``dual'' space $Y$ in place of $V$.  In this sense the Peterson elements of $V$ are the images of the
canonical elements $\sum_{d(R)=n}(-1)^{|R|}\binom{|R|}{R}\xi^R\in\A_*(k)$ under
the Dickson map of the dual space.  Equation~\eqref{eq:F2}, with the adjoint
$T^*$, suggests that this duality is the natural setting for these
formulas.

\emph{(c) Comparison with Arnon.}  For $p=2$, Arnon~\cite[\S\S4--5]{Arnon}
studies a different kind of duality.  He forms the inverse limit
$\widetilde D_\infty$ of the perfect Dickson algebras over $k$, and a
nondegenerate symmetric pairing between its bounded part and
$\widetilde D_\infty$ itself.  The pairing makes both into dual topological
Hopf algebras.  In it the elements $\omega_n$ are dual to the powers of
$\omega_1$, the coproduct is $\psi(\omega_n)=\sum_{i+j=n}\omega_i\otimes\omega_j$,
and the left action of a completed Steenrod algebra is dual to a right action
that he computes explicitly.  This construction is not used in the code, and
\code{DicksonDualSteenrodAlgebra} is unrelated to it.
